Pennsylvania Has 95,000 Amish — So Why Does Wisconsin Have MORE Amish Settlements?
If you asked most Americans to point to the center of Amish life, they would probably choose Pennsylvania.
And the numbers seem to prove them right.
In 2025, Pennsylvania had an estimated 95,410 Amish residents.
Wisconsin had only 27,535.
So Pennsylvania has well over three times as many Amish people.
But now look at the map in a different way.
Pennsylvania has 63 Amish settlements.
Wisconsin has 68.
Five more.
Wisconsin actually has more separate Amish communities than the state most closely associated with the Amish in the American imagination.
And that is not even the strangest part.
One Amish settlement around Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, contains roughly 44,765 people.
That single settlement is larger than the entire Amish population of Wisconsin.
Yet Wisconsin still has more settlements statewide.
So what exactly are we counting?
And why would 27,000 Amish people spread themselves across more separate communities than 95,000 people in Pennsylvania?
The answer is not that Wisconsin somehow became “more Amish” than Pennsylvania.
It is that these two states represent two completely different ways an Amish population can grow.
Pennsylvania built giants.
Wisconsin built dots.
And once you understand why, the entire map of Amish America starts to look different.
The first thing to understand is that an Amish settlement is not the same thing as a town.
It does not necessarily have a mayor.
It may not have a sign.
It may cross county lines.
And sometimes an English-speaking neighbor living five miles away might not even realize that researchers consider the Amish families around him part of a distinct settlement.
Researchers at the Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies define a settlement as a geographic Amish community containing one or more church districts.
And the church district is the more important building block.
An Amish church district generally contains around 20 to 40 households.
There is normally no giant church building where hundreds or thousands of Amish gather together every Sunday.
Worship usually takes place in homes, barns or other local spaces.
That limits how large a congregation can comfortably become.
So when enough families accumulate in one area, the church district divides.
One becomes two.
Two become three.
Three become ten.
But all ten can still belong to the same settlement if they remain geographically connected.
That distinction explains almost everything.
A settlement tells you:
How many geographically separate Amish communities exist?
A church district tells you much more about:
How large those communities have become internally.
And suddenly Pennsylvania and Wisconsin stop looking like a contradiction.
Pennsylvania has 636 church districts.
Wisconsin has only 209.
So Wisconsin wins on the number of separate dots.
Pennsylvania absolutely dominates in how much of the Amish population is packed inside those dots.
The best example is Lancaster.
The Lancaster County-area Amish settlement is one of the oldest continuously existing Amish communities in North America.
In 2025, researchers estimated it at roughly:
44,765 people.
And 267 church districts.
Pause on that second number.
The entire state of Wisconsin has only 209 districts.
Meaning Lancaster's single giant geographic settlement contains more individual Amish congregations than every Amish settlement in Wisconsin combined.
One Pennsylvania dot on the map contains more church districts than sixty-eight Wisconsin dots.

The Mifflin County area has around 36 districts and more than 5,500 Amish residents.
The Indiana County area has around 25 districts.
Pennsylvania has several old communities where generation after generation has accumulated in roughly the same geographic zone.
This is what a mature Amish settlement looks like.
It does not necessarily create a new settlement every time the population grows.
Often, it simply keeps dividing internally.
A new district forms.
Then another.
The map still shows one settlement.
But underneath that single dot, the population is multiplying.
This is why simply counting settlements can fool you.
If you looked only at dots, Lancaster counts as one.
A brand-new Amish community with fifteen people somewhere else can also count as one.
On a map, both receive a dot.
In reality, they are completely different demographic worlds.
Wisconsin has some substantial Amish communities too.
Cashton has grown into the state's largest settlement.
Kingston is another major center.
Augusta, Hillsboro, Wilton-Tomah and several others have existed for decades.
But most of Wisconsin's map is not one enormous community.
It is a network of smaller ones.
In 2025, Wisconsin's 27,535 Amish residents were spread across 68 settlements.
That works out to roughly 405 people per settlement, if you use a simple statewide average.
Pennsylvania averages more than 1,500 people per settlement.
The church-district comparison is even clearer.
Pennsylvania averages roughly 10 districts per settlement.
Wisconsin averages only about three.
And many Wisconsin communities are far smaller than that average suggests.
In Adams County, a settlement established in 2023 was estimated at only about 40 people.
Camp Douglas, founded in 2024, was estimated at roughly 15.
Warrens, established in 2023, around 20.
Other settlements consist of one district and perhaps a few dozen or a few hundred people.
At the same time, older Wisconsin communities like Cashton contain thousands.
So Wisconsin is not one unified Amish belt.
It is more like an archipelago.
Large communities.
Medium communities.
Tiny communities.
Some are only a few years old.
All separated enough geographically to count as different settlements.
And that raises the real question:
Why did Wisconsin develop this way?
The answer starts with movement.
Wisconsin's Amish map was built in waves.
Some early settlements appeared more than a century ago.
But several failed.
Families moved away.
Ministers left.
Communities disappeared.
Then new groups arrived later and started again in different places.
The modern Wisconsin Amish landscape really accelerated during the second half of the twentieth century.
Cashton dates to the 1960s.
Wilton-Tomah also emerged around that period.
Kingston took shape in the 1970s.
Augusta followed.
Then came communities established during the 1980s, 1990s, 2000s, 2010s and even the 2020s.
In other words, Wisconsin did not receive one giant migration that settled in one valley and stayed there.
Different Amish groups arrived at different times, often from different states and different religious affiliations.
That matters because “the Amish” are not one centrally controlled organization.
There is no national Amish headquarters deciding:
“This county gets 500 families.”
Individual church communities differ in how they regulate technology, occupations, farming practices and other details of daily life.
Families often prefer to settle near other Amish whose church practices are compatible with their own.
So when Amish migration enters a state, it does not necessarily merge into one existing community.
Sometimes the better fit is somewhere else.
A different county.
A different cluster.
A different settlement.
And Wisconsin has become a place where many of those separate streams have landed.
There is a revealing example from 1990.
A group of Amish men from the Spring Run area of Pennsylvania traveled west to inspect Wisconsin.
According to a recent academic study of Wisconsin Amish migration, they were looking for two very practical things:
less expensive farms and a workable milk market.
They liked the area around Athens, Wisconsin.
A new settlement followed.
That story is small, but the mechanism is enormous.
For a young Amish family trying to establish a farm, land is not simply an investment.
It can determine whether the lifestyle is economically possible at all.
When an old settlement grows for generations, farmland gets divided, purchased, inherited and surrounded by development.
More sons and daughters reach adulthood.
More households need somewhere to live.
But the amount of land does not multiply with the population.
Eventually, some families look outward.
Not because the old community is dying.
Often because it is doing the opposite.
It has produced more families than the local land market can easily absorb.
And Wisconsin has repeatedly offered something older Amish regions could not offer as easily:
rural agricultural areas where a new group could establish itself separately.
But cheap land is only one part of the story.
The Young Center lists several reasons Amish communities establish new settlements.
Affordable farmland.
Access to suitable nonfarm work.
Rural isolation.
A local social and physical environment compatible with their way of life.
Proximity to relatives or similar church groups.
And sometimes the need to resolve internal church or leadership disagreements.
That means two families can leave the same old settlement for completely different reasons.
And two groups arriving in Wisconsin may intentionally settle in different places.
More migration does not always make one dot bigger.
Sometimes it creates another dot.
The movement is still happening.
A 2025 study examining household relocations into Wisconsin Amish settlements found that newcomers were not simply shifting around inside the state.
Among households in the study that reported a prior location, a majority of those moving into Wisconsin settlements had come from another state.
A large share also moved from one Wisconsin settlement to another.
And the patterns were not random.
Religious affiliation appeared to be an important factor in deciding which settlement families chose.
That is critical.
Imagine Wisconsin not as one destination, but as dozens of potential destinations.
A family does not simply ask:
“Should we move to Wisconsin?”
They may ask:
Which community has people we know?
Which church practices match ours?
Where is land available?
Where can I make a living?
Where are my relatives?
Where can my children eventually find spouses within a compatible community?
Those decisions create a decentralized migration network.
One family goes first.
Relatives visit.
Another property becomes available.
A second household moves.
Then a third.
Eventually there are enough families to support regular worship.
A school may appear.
Businesses follow.
A tiny cluster becomes a functioning settlement.
And the map gets another dot.
If you're enjoying stories about how communities quietly reshape the map, subscribe—because this pattern goes much further than Pennsylvania and Wisconsin.
Now compare what happened from 2015 to 2025.
Pennsylvania went from 54 settlements to 63.
A net gain of nine.
But its church districts exploded from 465 to 636.
An increase of 171 districts.
That is classic internal growth.
Existing communities are getting much bigger.
Wisconsin followed a different path.
It went from 50 settlements to 68.
A gain of eighteen.
Twice Pennsylvania's net settlement increase.
Its church districts also grew—from 138 to 209—but the geographic spread is the striking part.
Wisconsin kept adding separate communities while Pennsylvania's existing giants continued dividing internally.
Put another way:
Over that decade, Pennsylvania added roughly one new settlement for every nineteen additional church districts.
Wisconsin added one new settlement for roughly every four additional districts.
Those are not exact rules for how communities form.
But the contrast reveals the shape of growth.
Pennsylvania's growth is heavily concentrated inside established communities.
Wisconsin is much more geographically distributed.
And Wisconsin is not even the only state doing this.
Missouri had 64 settlements in 2025.
New York had 60.
Kentucky had 55.
Michigan had 53.
Ohio led them all with 74.
The old mental picture of Amish America as Pennsylvania, Ohio and Indiana is becoming less useful every year.
Those states still contain most of the people.
But the settlement frontier is much wider.
There is one important caution here.
A settlement is a research category.
It is not a permanent legal boundary carved into stone.
Researchers sometimes reconsider where one settlement ends and another begins.
That happened recently around Lancaster.
For years, church districts in southeastern York County were counted as part of the Lancaster-area settlement.
As the York County community developed more of its own identity, researchers began listing it separately.
Nothing dramatic happened overnight.
Thousands of people did not suddenly move.
The classification changed because the community itself had changed enough to be treated as geographically distinct.
That tells us something important.
The exact difference between 68 Wisconsin settlements and 63 Pennsylvania settlements should not be treated like a sports score.
Five is not a magical number.
Next year's totals may change.
Communities can begin.
They can fail.
They can divide.
Researchers can refine boundaries.
The meaningful fact is not simply:
Wisconsin 68.
Pennsylvania 63.
The meaningful fact is the structure underneath those numbers.
One state contains many more people concentrated in giant old settlements.
The other spreads a much smaller population across a remarkable number of independent communities.
That pattern is real even if an individual boundary changes.
And there is another reason this matters.
The Amish population of North America is not stable.
It is growing rapidly.
In 2000, researchers estimated about 177,910 Amish people.
By 2025, the estimate had reached roughly 410,955.
An increase of about 131%.
The population roughly doubles every twenty years.
The main reasons are unusually large families and a high percentage of young people who eventually join the church as adults.
That creates a simple geographic problem.
Every generation needs more households than the generation before it.
Some communities absorb those households internally.
That is Pennsylvania.
More homes.
More businesses.
More church districts.
Existing settlement gets denser and larger.
But there is a limit to how much expansion can happen in one place.
Land becomes difficult to obtain.
Jobs change.
Development closes in.
Or families simply prefer another environment.
Then the pressure escapes outward.
A few households leave.
And if enough compatible families follow, the community does not disappear.
It reproduces somewhere else.
That is why the Amish map expands differently from the map of many other growing populations.
Growth does not have to produce a giant city.
It can create another settlement.
Then another.
Then another.
In 2025 alone, researchers recorded nine newly established Amish settlements across North America, while no existing settlement dissolved during that one-year period.
And more than half of all Amish settlements contain only a single church district.
That means most dots on the map are not Lancaster.
They are small.
Some fragile.
Some new.
Some may not survive.
But a few of them could eventually become the next Cashton.
The next Kingston.
Or, generations from now, something much larger.
Now go back to the original numbers.
Pennsylvania:
about 95,410 Amish people.
63 settlements.
636 church districts.
Wisconsin:
about 27,535 people.
68 settlements.
209 districts.
Pennsylvania has more than three times the Amish population.
Almost three times the number of church districts.
Yet Wisconsin has five more settlements.
The numbers no longer contradict each other.
They describe two different stages of community development.
Pennsylvania is filled with older, deeper roots.
Wisconsin contains more separate shoots.
Pennsylvania's average settlement holds roughly 1,500 people.
Wisconsin's roughly 400.
Pennsylvania's typical map dot, taken statewide, represents a much larger concentration.
Wisconsin needs many more dots to represent far fewer people.
And that is why the map feels wrong at first.
We unconsciously assume one settlement means one similar-sized community.
It doesn't.
A settlement can mean fifteen people in a new rural cluster.
Or nearly forty-five thousand people around Lancaster.
Both are counted once.
So why does Wisconsin have more Amish settlements than Pennsylvania?
Not because Wisconsin has more Amish.
It doesn't.
Not because Pennsylvania is losing its importance.
It isn't.
And not because Amish families are abandoning Pennsylvania in one giant migration.
The real answer is more interesting.
Pennsylvania shows what happens when Amish communities grow deep.
Wisconsin shows what happens when they grow wide.
In Pennsylvania, generations have piled into some of the oldest and largest Amish settlements on Earth.
One geographic community can contain hundreds of congregations.
In Wisconsin, different waves of families have repeatedly found new rural areas, formed separate communities and created a much more fragmented map.
Pennsylvania built giants.
Wisconsin built dots.
And the smallest dots may be the ones worth watching.
Because Lancaster was once a small settlement too.
Cashton was once a handful of families.
Kingston was once new.
Every giant Amish community began with someone making the same basic decision:
There is not enough room for our future here.
Let's try somewhere else.
A farm is purchased.
Another family follows.
A congregation forms.
Children are born.
The district divides.
And if enough people remain long enough, one tiny mark on a map becomes a community that lasts for generations.
That is the real meaning behind Wisconsin's sixty-eight settlements.
The Amish population is not only getting larger.
It is spreading into more versions of itself.
And that leaves an even more interesting question.
If a handful of Amish families find cheap land somewhere new tomorrow…
what exactly has to happen before they can call it a real Amish settlement?
Because buying the farms is only the beginning.
