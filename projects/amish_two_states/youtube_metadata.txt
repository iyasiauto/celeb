DESCRIPTION
Pennsylvania had an estimated 95,410 Amish residents in 2025. Wisconsin had 27,535 — barely more
than a quarter as many. Yet Wisconsin has 68 Amish settlements and Pennsylvania has 63.

The contradiction disappears once you know what a settlement actually is. It is not a town. It has
no mayor, often no sign, and it can cross county lines. Researchers at the Young Center for
Anabaptist and Pietist Studies define it as a geographic Amish community containing one or more
church districts — and the church district, 20 to 40 households worshipping in each other's homes,
is the real building block. When a district fills up it divides: one becomes two, two become three,
three become ten, and the map still shows a single dot.

That is Pennsylvania. The Lancaster County settlement alone holds roughly 44,765 people and 267
church districts — more congregations than every Amish settlement in Wisconsin combined. Wisconsin
grew the other way: wave after wave of families from different states and different affiliations
landing in different counties, so its 27,535 people are spread across 68 separate communities,
some of them barely fifteen or twenty people old.

This video follows the whole argument: what a settlement is, how Lancaster became a giant, why
Wisconsin became an archipelago, the 1990 trip from Spring Run that ended in Athens, the arithmetic
of land that pushes families out of old settlements, what the 2015–2025 decade did to both states,
and why the Amish population — 177,910 in 2000, about 410,955 in 2025 — keeps turning growth into
new dots on the map instead of one larger city.

Sources: Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies (Elizabethtown College) settlement and
church-district estimates, 2015–2025; a 2025 study of household relocations into Wisconsin Amish
settlements.
