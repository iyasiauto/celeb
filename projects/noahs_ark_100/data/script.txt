One hundred percent.

That's the number a researcher gave the Daily Mail on September 22nd, 2026, standing in front of a boat-shaped rock formation in eastern Turkey. His team, he said, was "one hundred percent" certain that this formation — the Durupınar site, near Mount Ararat — is man-made. Not probably. Not likely. One hundred percent.

Within hours, the internet translated that quote the way the internet always does. Headlines appeared asking whether Noah's Ark had finally been confirmed. Videos racked up millions of views claiming scientists had proven the ark exists. Comment sections filled with celebration, with mockery, and with arguments about faith and science that have been running, in one form or another, for centuries.

But here's the thing about the number one hundred percent.

Science doesn't actually work in percentages like that. No laboratory certifies a discovery "one hundred percent confirmed." No peer-reviewed journal stamps certainty onto a claim. Real confirmation is a process — evidence, replication, independent verification — and it takes years. Sometimes decades.

So when a lead researcher declares one hundred percent certainty before a single laboratory result has come back — and that's exactly when this quote was given, one day before his team's drilling program even began — a responsible investigator has to ask a different question.

Not "is the ark confirmed?"

But this: what has actually been found at this site? What has genuinely been confirmed? And what is still a claim wearing the costume of a conclusion?

That's what this video is going to do. Not mock the claim. Not sell it either. We're going to walk through every pillar of evidence at the Durupınar formation — the dimensions, the history, the scans, the soil, and the brand-new drilling that started the day after that quote — and for each one, we're going to separate three things that headlines love to blur together: what was observed, what was claimed, and what has actually been confirmed.

If you're the kind of viewer who wants the truth to be more interesting than the headline, consider subscribing — because that's the standard we hold every story to here.

Let's start with the word at the center of all of this: confirmed.

When you hear that something has been "confirmed," you're picturing a specific sequence of events. Researchers make a claim. Outside experts test it. Laboratories analyze physical evidence. Results get published, attacked, defended, and replicated. And only then — after people with no stake in the outcome agree the evidence holds — does something move from "claimed" to "confirmed."

None of that has happened at Durupınar. Not one step of it, as of late September 2026.

What has happened is this. A research organization called Noah's Ark Scans, led by an American named Andrew Jones, has spent years scanning, sampling, and now drilling into a formation its founder openly believes is the ark. In his own words, published on his project's website: "I believe this is Noah's Ark." And this September, the team began the first deep core drilling ever permitted at the site — working with Turkish archaeologists from Sivas Cumhuriyet University, led by Professor Cenker Atila, under official authorization from Turkish government agencies.

That expedition is real. The permits are real. The university partnership is real. The core samples now sitting in cold storage are real.

And every single headline you've seen about this story — the "one hundred percent" quote, the drilling, the shattered drill bit you may have heard about, the whispers of petrified wood — all of it flows from that one team, through its own press releases and interviews. There is, at this moment, no second source. No independent laboratory. No outside confirmation of anything interpretive.

Keep that architecture in mind, because now we're going to walk through the evidence itself. And some of it is genuinely intriguing.

Pillar one: the shape and the size.

The Durupınar formation is a long, symmetrical outline on the slopes of Mount Tendürek, about twenty-nine kilometers south of the summit of Greater Mount Ararat, near Turkey's border with Iran. From the air, it looks astonishingly like the hull of an ancient ship — a pointed end, a rounded end, raised edges running the length of it. Turkish army cartographer Captain İlhan Durupınar spotted it in aerial photographs in 1959, during a NATO mapping mission, and the site has carried his name ever since.

Its most-cited length is roughly 157 meters — about 515 feet.

And here is the fact that has powered sixty-six years of fascination: in the Book of Genesis, the ark's length is given as three hundred cubits. If you convert that using the Egyptian royal cubit — about 20.6 inches — three hundred cubits comes out to almost exactly 515 feet.

The match is real. The numbers are what they are. And supporters of the site treat this as a foundation stone of the entire case.

But let's do what "confirmed" actually requires, and stress-test it.

First problem: a cubit was not a fixed unit. Different ancient cultures used different lengths. Use a shorter common cubit, and the biblical ark shrinks to around 450 feet — a much worse match. So the famous 515-foot correspondence depends entirely on which ancient ruler you choose to measure with.

Second problem: width. Fifty royal cubits — the biblical width — comes out to about 86 feet. The formation measures roughly 138 feet across at its middle. That's not a match; it's off by more than half. Supporters answer that a wooden hull collapsing for millennia would flatten and splay outward. That's possible. But notice the logic: when a measurement matches, it's evidence. When it doesn't, there's an explanation for why it doesn't. A theory that can absorb every outcome — match or mismatch — can never actually be tested. And an untestable theory can never be confirmed. That's not cynicism; that's just how confirmation works.

There's also the location itself. Genesis says the ark came to rest "on the mountains of Ararat" — a region, not a single peak, so defenders of the site argue a formation twenty-nine kilometers from the summit still qualifies. But critics raise a specific difficulty: the formation lies low, inside a valley roughly a thousand meters deep. In the Genesis account, the ark grounds while the surrounding mountaintops remain submerged for months afterward. A vessel settling into the bottom of a deep valley, skeptics argue, is hard to square with that picture.

So pillar one gives us this verdict: a genuinely striking shape, a length that matches under one specific cubit standard, a width that doesn't, and a location both sides argue over. Observed: yes. Confirmed as a ship: no.

And before we go further, it's worth being precise about what the religious texts actually say — because precision here is a form of respect.

In the Islamic tradition, the flood of Prophet Nuh carries enormous theological weight. The Qur'an describes him building a vessel under divine guidance, and it says the ark came to rest on "Al-Judi" — a location scholars have debated for centuries, with various mountains proposed across the region. In the Biblical tradition, Genesis describes the gopher wood, the pitch, the rooms, the dimensions we've just discussed. These are two great textual traditions with their own details, their own vocabularies, their own scholarly histories.

And here is what neither of them contains: any mention of the Durupınar formation. The connection between this specific hillside and the ark is not found in scripture. It was made by modern researchers, in the modern era, with modern instruments. That's not an insult to the connection — it's simply where it comes from, and honesty about origins matters in both science and faith.

Which brings us to the history of that connection — because this hillside has been through this cycle before.

The first serious expedition came in 1960, just a year after the aerial photos surfaced. An American-led team — which included surveyor Arthur Brandenberger, a man who personally believed the ark could be found — traveled to the site, dug into it, and even used dynamite to test what lay beneath the surface. Their official conclusion: no visible archaeological remains. A freak of nature. Case closed.

For a while, it was.

Then, in 1977, the site was rediscovered and promoted by a man named Ron Wyatt. Wyatt was not an archaeologist or a geologist — he was a nurse anesthetist from Tennessee who devoted his life and savings to biblical archaeology. Over the years he claimed to have found not only Noah's Ark, but the Ark of the Covenant, the true site of Mount Sinai, and chariot wheels on the floor of the Red Sea. At Durupınar, he claimed the raised edges of the formation were petrified hull timbers, that his metal detectors had found iron fittings, and that massive carved stones sitting miles away were the ark's anchor stones, dropped as the vessel approached land.

To his supporters, Wyatt was a courageous pioneer ignored by institutions. To mainstream science, he was a sincere amateur whose claims collapsed under scrutiny. And here's the detail most retellings quietly leave out: Wyatt's own expedition partner eventually changed his mind. David Fasold, a marine salvage expert who worked alongside Wyatt through the 1980s, concluded the site was a natural formation. In 1996, he co-authored a geology paper arguing exactly that. And in 1997, under oath in an Australian courtroom, Fasold described the claim that Noah's Ark had been found as — his words — "absolute BS."

The man who helped launch the modern Durupınar ark theory ended up testifying against it.

That's the ground the 2026 expedition is standing on. Not a blank slate. A site with sixty-six years of contested history, where previous generations of instruments produced the same repeating cycle: an anomaly, a headline, and a geological explanation. The current team knows this history. Their entire strategy — better technology, official permits, actual cores — is built to break that cycle.

So let's look at what that technology found.

Pillar two: the scans.

Over recent years, the Noah's Ark Scans team has run a serious technological battery over this mountainside. Ground-penetrating radar, which sends radio pulses into the earth and reads the echoes that bounce back. Electrical resistivity tomography, which maps how easily current moves through underground material. LiDAR surface mapping. Thermal imaging. Audio-magnetotelluric surveys reaching, the team says, as deep as three hundred meters.

And the team reports remarkable results: long straight lines underground, right-angle intersections, layered horizontal tiers, and elongated voids — features they interpret as decks, corridors, and chambers. In interviews, team members have described the formation as behaving "like a structure, not like rock."

This is the pillar that produces the viral radar images — the ones that look, to an untrained eye, like architectural blueprints of a buried ship.

Now here's what ground-penetrating radar actually does. It detects boundaries — places where underground material changes: soil to stone, solid to void, dry to wet. It can absolutely reveal straight lines and right angles. But here's the part that never makes the thumbnails: straight lines and right angles are not a human signature. Limestone fractures along planar joints — clean, straight, often perpendicular. Geologists encounter right angles in natural rock constantly. A radar return can tell you a boundary exists. It cannot tell you who built the boundary — or whether anyone did.

Think of it like an X-ray. An X-ray can show you a shape inside the body. It takes a trained radiologist — and often a biopsy — to say what that shape actually is. The scans at Durupınar are the X-ray. The cores are the biopsy. And the biopsy results aren't back yet.

There's also a second limitation, maybe the most important one in this entire video. Those radar images you see online are interpretations of raw data. The raw data has never been released for independent geophysicists to examine. Every rendered image, every colored cross-section showing "decks," has been produced by the same team that believes the ark is there.

That doesn't make the scans fake. It makes them unverified. And unverified is the opposite of confirmed.

Pillar three: the soil.

In 2024, the project collected 88 soil samples — some from inside the boat-shaped outline, some from the surrounding terrain. According to the team, the interior soil contains roughly three times more organic matter and about 38 percent more potassium than the soil outside. Their interpretation: you're looking at the chemical ghost of an enormous wooden structure, rotted into the earth over thousands of years. They also point out that grass above the formation grows greener and denser than the slopes around it — visible from the air, like a green ship floating in brown terrain.

Of all the project's claims, this one at least has the shape of testable science: samples, numbers, a comparison group.

But then you have to ask the confirmation question again: has anyone outside the project examined these results? Has the lab work been published with methods and raw data? As of today — no. The figures come from the team's own announcements, repeated by friendly media. One source, many echoes.

And there's a competing explanation that requires no ark at all. Look at the shape again: an elongated oval with raised edges. That's a bowl. Bowls collect rainwater. And here's the geological detail that matters — different rock weathers into different soil. Geologist Andrew Snelling, who examined this exact claim, points out that a depression collecting moisture over different underlying rock will naturally support richer vegetation and accumulate more organic content. In other words: greener grass and organic-rich soil inside a bowl-shaped catchment is what you'd expect whether there's a ship underneath or not.

Now — here's a detail about Snelling that should stop you in your tracks, because it changes what this criticism means. Andrew Snelling is not a secular debunker. He is a young-earth creationist geologist. He works for Answers in Genesis — the organization that believes the flood literally happened and built a full-size ark attraction in Kentucky. This is a man whose worldview would love for the ark to be found.

And he rejects the Durupınar site. His organization has publicly concluded that nothing presented there moves beyond claims made — and refuted — decades ago. When the people most motivated to believe your discovery look at your evidence and walk away, "confirmed" is not the word you're left with.

Pillar four: the drilling.

This is the newest evidence — and the reason this story exploded in September 2026.

For sixty years, nobody had properly drilled this formation. A 1988 attempt produced cores the current team says were contaminated by the water used to cool the drill bit. Surface observation and scanning were all anyone had. Then, on September 23rd, 2026 — one day after Jones's "one hundred percent" quote — the team announced that deep coring had begun: a dry percussion system, no drilling fluids, pulling intact cores from as deep as eighteen meters. Nearly sixty feet down.

And according to the project's own press release, the first holes produced genuinely interesting material: layered sediments, organic-rich intervals, multiple underground cavities — one of which kept filling with water during drilling, a feature the team informally started calling a "room."

And in one borehole, inside the boat-shaped outline, at roughly four to five meters depth, the drill hit a layer so hard it shattered the bit.

Jones's quote: whatever the drill struck was "harder than limestone." His suggested possibilities, again in his own words: "petrified or highly mineralized wood" — to be investigated, he emphasized, through third-party laboratory testing.

Now. If you watched this story on social media, you probably saw the compressed version: "Drill breaks on petrified wood inside Noah's Ark." But walk it back through the confirmation filter. What was observed? A hard layer at four to five meters — reported by the project. What was claimed? Possibly petrified wood — stated as a hope, not a result. What has been confirmed? Nothing. No laboratory has touched it. And this is volcanic terrain — the published geology of this region describes basalts, andesites, and dense altered rock throughout the area. Drill bits break on natural volcanic rock every working day in this part of the world. A shattered bit is a genuine event. It is not a species identification.

Same discipline for the "room." A water-filled cavity was encountered — that's the observation. But limestone dissolves in groundwater; it's how caves form all over the world. And geologist Murat Avci's peer-reviewed 2007 study of this very site describes the formation as a slumped block of Miocene limestone whose voids and tunnels formed through exactly that dissolution process — what geologists call karst. Cavities in limestone are the expectation, not the anomaly. Calling one a "room" is an interpretation layered on top — by people hoping to find rooms.

So run the tally across all four pillars. Observations: real, and in some cases genuinely intriguing. Claims: bold, sometimes extraordinary. Confirmations: zero. Not because the evidence has been disproven — but because the process that confirmation actually means hasn't happened yet.

Which raises an uncomfortable question. If nothing has been confirmed, where did "one hundred percent" come from?

It came from conviction. Jones has been visiting this site since 1997 — nearly thirty years. He moved to Turkey to be near it. His organization fundraises around it. He is, by every account, sincere. But sincerity and certainty are not the same thing. And here's the asymmetry worth noticing: his one hundred percent was declared on September 22nd. The drilling — the first physical test that could actually confirm or kill the claim — began September 23rd. Certainty arrived one day before the evidence. That's not how confirmation works. That's how belief works. And belief, it's worth saying clearly, doesn't need a rock formation to be valid — but a rock formation needs more than belief to be confirmed.

Now, in the spirit of honesty, let's do the other side justice — because the skeptical case is not invincible either, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of distortion.

The mainstream geological explanation comes in two published forms. Geologist Lorence Collins, who examined rock samples from the site under a microscope, describes folded, volcanic-origin sediments — a structure called a doubly plunging syncline, essentially a trough-shaped fold tilted at both ends — later scoured into a boat-like profile by a clay-rich landslide flowing around it. He identified Wyatt's famous "iron fittings" as natural concretions of limonite and magnetite, minerals groundwater deposits in rock with no human involvement. The supposed petrified wood, in his examination, was crinkled metamorphosed peridotite — a common rock in this volcanic region. And the anchor stones were carved, he found, from local andesite — the same stone as the surrounding mountains, not material carried from anywhere else. Avci's 2007 study, as we heard, describes a slumped limestone block refined by earthflow, ice melt, and dissolution.

That's the published, peer-reviewed position. But the current team raises points a fair-minded viewer should hear. The formation's symmetry is striking; natural earthflows are usually messier. The mudflow-around-a-boulder theory has a fluid-dynamics problem the project's defenders like to raise: in their reading, the pointed end of the "ship" faces uphill, where they'd argue a blunt obstruction should sit. And the team's drilling, they say, has so far encountered no continuous bedrock inside the formation — which they present as contradicting the solid-rock models.

Are those kill shots against the geological explanation? No. Symmetry can emerge from folded strata; doubly plunging synclines produce exactly this kind of lens shape. The "no bedrock" point cuts both ways until the cores are published — a slumped block riding on clay, precisely what Avci described, wouldn't read as in-situ bedrock either. But these are real arguments, made by people who have stood on the ground and drilled it, and they deserve engagement rather than eye-rolling. That's what makes this a genuine controversy rather than a simple hoax story: the dispute is over interpretation of real observations, and some of the interpretive questions are legitimately open.

So what would actually confirm this? Not a quote. Not a radar image. What would it take?

The roadmap is refreshingly concrete, and for the first time in this site's history, it's actually underway.

First: microscopy on the cores. If petrified wood exists in that formation, it will still carry its cellular architecture — growth rings, vessel structures — visible under a microscope. When wood petrifies, minerals replace the organic tissue cell by cell, preserving its anatomy in stone. That is a yes-or-no answer, not a matter of opinion.

Second: radiocarbon dating on the organic-rich layers. If the carbon dates to a few centuries, or matches the surrounding sediments, the ark interpretation is finished. If it came back around four to five thousand years old, skeptics would face a genuinely hard problem — though even then, ancient carbon is not automatically a ship.

Third: independent replication. Outside geologists and geophysicists, with no stake in the outcome, given the raw scan data and the cores, free to test competing hypotheses.

And fourth: peer-reviewed publication — methods open, data open, conclusions exposed to attack by people who aren't fundraising on the answer.

The team says all of this is planned: compositional, organic, and stratigraphic testing through the winter of 2026 into 2027, with a public scientific statement to follow. They also intend to send a custom-built camera drone — they've named it GOPHER, after the gopher wood of Genesis — into the radar-mapped voids, to film whatever is down there. If that drone ever returns images of worked timber — joinery, beams, deliberate construction — everything changes. If it returns dissolved limestone passages, the geology stands. Either outcome is possible. But as of today, the drone has not flown. The labs have not reported. The journals have not published.

Noah's Ark — one hundred percent confirmed?

No. Not by the standard the word "confirmed" actually means. Not by any laboratory, any independent scientist, any published paper. The "one hundred percent" is one sincere man's statement of belief, made one day before his first real physical test began. Strip away the headline, and here's what researchers actually found at Durupınar: a genuinely permitted, university-supervised expedition — the first of its kind. Real cores from nearly sixty feet down. Organic-rich layers, now awaiting testing. Underground cavities consistent with dissolved limestone — or, the team hopes, something more. A hard layer at four to five meters that broke a drill bit, still unidentified. Scans showing boundaries that could be geology or could be structure. Soil with elevated organics that a bowl-shaped catchment over limestone might explain all by itself. And a sixty-six-year-old controversy that has, for the first time, walked itself into a laboratory.

That's the honest state of play. It's less than "confirmed." And it's more than "nothing."

And maybe that's the real story here. For decades, this debate was fought with photographs and passion. Ron Wyatt claimed petrified timbers in the seventies; his own partner ended up denouncing the claim under oath. Geologists published; believers believed; nothing moved, because nothing was testable. Now, finally, there are physical samples in cold storage and a testing timeline. Whatever you hope is down there, that shift — from assertion to evidence — deserves respect. Even the researchers' fiercest critics should want those lab results published. Even their most devoted supporters should want them examined by independent hands.

Because the story of Noah — of Prophet Nuh — has survived for millennia as a matter of faith, not microscopy. It asks for belief, not for a core sample.
